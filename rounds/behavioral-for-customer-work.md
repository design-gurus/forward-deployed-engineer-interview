# Behavioral Questions for Customer Work

FDE behavioral questions ask for evidence that a candidate can be trusted alone with a customer. They cover motivation, difficult stakeholders, declining scope, failure in production, and carrying feedback to the product team. In several loops these questions are not limited to one round: at Palantir they are commonly reported to take 15 to 20 minutes of each technical round.

## The five question families

| Family | Sample question | What the interviewer listens for |
|---|---|---|
| Motivation | "Why forward deployed work and not a product team?" | A specific reason tied to customers, not a wish to avoid deep engineering |
| Difficult customers | "Tell me about a stakeholder who disagreed with your plan." | The other person's reason, stated fairly, and what changed |
| Scope and deadlines | "Describe a time you said no to a request." | Costs and options offered, not a refusal |
| Failure in production | "Tell me about something you shipped that broke." | A precise account, the cost, and the change afterward |
| Field feedback | "When did customer work change what a product team built?" | A specific finding, who received it, and what happened |

## Two answers to prepare separately

"Why FDE?" and "Why this company?" are different questions. The first is about the kind of work. The second is about the product, the customers, and the mission. A candidate who gives one answer to both is commonly reported to be asked the second one again.

## Structure for a story

STAR is a four-part answer structure: Situation, Task, Action, Result. For customer work, add two details that interviewers ask for when they are missing.

| Part | Content | Share of the answer |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | The customer, the user, and the number that defines the problem | 15 percent |
| Task | What you personally were responsible for | 10 percent |
| Action | What you did and said, including the exact words in a hard conversation | 50 percent |
| Result | A number, with a before and an after | 15 percent |
| What the customer said | Their reaction, quoted or paraphrased | 5 percent |
| What you would change | One thing, stated plainly | 5 percent |

## Weak and strong, on declining scope

A weak answer:

> "The customer wanted more features, so I aligned the team and managed expectations, and we delivered on time."

A strong answer:

> "In week four of a six-week engagement, the customer's operations lead asked us to add injury claims to the pilot. I told her that injury claims would need about two more weeks of testing, because an error there costs far more. I offered two options: keep the date with glass and minor damage only, or move the date by two weeks and include injury. She chose the first, and we added injury claims in a second phase a month later. Looking back, I should have raised the question in week one instead of waiting for her to ask."

Interviewers follow up on exact words. A phrase such as "I aligned the team" leads to the question "What did you actually say?" The strong answer has already answered it.

## The values conversation

At Anthropic, a final panel on behavior and values is commonly reported as the hardest stage. Reported topics include a moral conflict, a time the candidate was wrong, and pressure from an executive. An honest view with one real point of disagreement is reported to score higher than polished enthusiasm. See [Anthropic](../companies/anthropic.md).

## A story bank of six

Prepare six stories, each with numbers: one on motivation, two on difficult stakeholders, one on declining scope, one failure, and one piece of feedback that changed a product. Six stories cover the five families with one spare.
